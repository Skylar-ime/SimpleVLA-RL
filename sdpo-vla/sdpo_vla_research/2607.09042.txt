Correspondence: irisxu@mit.edu 1 Introduction Reinforcement learning (RL) [ 8 ] is increasingly used to fine-tune vision-language-action (VLA) models for robot control [ 3 , 5 ] . A typical pipeline adapts a VLA to a new task by supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on a few teleoperated demonstrations, then continues with RL so that the robot improves from its own interaction. But when the initial policy rarely succeeds, most rollouts receive no reward and contribute little to learning, making RL fine-tuning highly sample inefficient. A failed rollout, however, is not necessarily useless data. In Figure 1 , every attempt at “Put the green container in the bowl” fails, yet one rollout puts the tape in the bowl and another moves the green container toward it. Each rollout succeeds at some task, so relabeling it with the instruction it did satisfy turns a failure into a success. These hindsight tasks are also building blocks of the target task: one placing an object in the bowl and the other moving the green container. Because VLAs generalize compositionally over familiar objects and skills [ 4 , 33 ] , learning these building blocks helps the policy put them together. This strategy, known as hindsight relabeling, is well established in goal-conditioned RL. Hindsight experience replay [ 1 ] relabels a trajectory that misses its goal with the outcome it actually reached, and later work extends the idea to richer goal spaces such as images [ 18 , 17 , 6 , 19 ] . In these settings, relabeling is straightforward, because the outcome a trajectory reached is directly observable and can serve as its goal. For a language-conditioned VLA, however, the instruction a rollout satisfied is not observed and must be inferred from the robot’s behavior. Prior language relabeling for robots operates offline, deriving new instructions for fixed datasets from existing annotations or primitive behaviors [ 28 , 7 , 23 ] . Inferring what a failed rollout accomplished while the policy is still being trained remains an open problem. Pretrained vision-language models (VLMs) [ 24 ] offer a natural way to infer what a robot accomplished. Given a rollout video, a VLM can describe the robot’s behavior in open-ended language, and this description can replace the rollout’s original instruction. Using these labels for RL fine-tuning, however, raises two challenges. First, a VLM asked to describe a rollout will usually produce some instruction, even when the robot only moved accidentally, and its labels can be poorly grounded in the scene. Second, relabeled rollouts are off-policy, because the policy produced them while following a different instruction, so the update must correct for this mismatch. We propose Learning from Hindsight (LfH), which relabels failed rollouts with a VLM during RL fine-tuning and addresses both challenges. For the first, LfH treats relabeling as a constrained judgment rather than free-form captioning. The VLM is grounded in the objects present in the scene and may answer that a rollout accomplished nothing. A separate query then checks whether rollouts actually complete the hindsight instruction and may answer unsure when the outcome is ambiguous, so a label that does not match the behavior earns little or no reward. In our ablation on LIBERO-PRO Goal, removing any of these options reduces the gains of LfH (Section A.1 ). For the second, the exact correction [ 18 ] multiplies probability ratios across every step of the trajectory, a product that vanishes or explodes over long VLA horizons. LfH instead corrects only for the state and action at each step, accepting a small bias in exchange for much lower variance (Section A.2 ). Our principal contribution is showing that VLMs can turn failed rollouts into useful training data for VLAs. On LIBERO-PRO [ 31 ] manipulation tasks whose instructions differ from those seen in demonstrations, LfH matches the final performance of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) [ 21 ] with approximately 5 × 5\times fewer rollouts. It also outperforms learned progress rewards [ 13 , 11 ] , which measure progress only on the commanded task. These gains hold across VLA backbones and extend to a real Franka robot, where, within 160 training rollouts, LfH raises success from 0 % 0\% to 56 % 56\% while GRPO reaches 22 % 22\% . Training on these hindsight tasks with RL teaches skills that the policy composes to solve the target task with fewer samples. 2 Related Work Hindsight and Language Relabeling. Hindsight Experience Replay (HER) [ 1 ] relabels a trajectory with the goal it actually achieved, and later work extends the idea to image goals [ 19 ] . Language relabeling has mostly been used to enlarge offline datasets. Zhang et al. [ 30 ] relabel agent trajectories in the Craftax game with an LLM, DIAL [ 23 ] and SPRINT [ 28 ] relabel robot datasets for policy learning and pretraining, and CAST [ 7 ] and InstructVLA [ 25 ] augment VLA training with counterfactual instructions and multimodal instruction tuning. SOAR [ 32 ] is closest in applying a VLM to autonomously collected robot data, where it proposes tasks and detects success, but it improves the policy with goal-conditioned behavior cloning rather than RL. In these works, relabeling adds supervision to existing data to broaden the range of instructions a policy can follow. LfH uses relabeling for a different purpose. It targets a task the policy cannot yet solve, relabels the policy’s own failures on that task during online RL, and trains on the tasks those failures already accomplish so that progress transfers back to the target. Learned Reward Models. Reward shaping provides learning signal when successes are rare by rewarding intermediate progress [ 16 ] . Recent work learns such rewards from visual data, as goal- or language-conditioned value functions [ 15 , 14 ] , from demonstrations [ 13 , 11 , 27 ] , or from preference labels [ 22 ] . These models refine how a rollout is scored under its commanded instruction. LfH instead changes which instruction the rollout is credited for, which requires inferring what it accomplished and lets it provide signal even when it makes no progress on the commanded task. 3 Preliminaries We study RL fine-tuning of VLA policies [ 3 , 9 ] . A GRPO group provides no learning signal when all its trajectories receive the same reward. A weak initial policy fails most rollouts, so many groups receive all-zero rewards. These groups have zero normalized advantage in Eq. ( 1 ), contribute no policy gradient, and fine-tuning makes little progress. LfH recovers learning signal from these otherwise discarded trajectories. Approach. A trajectory that fails under one instruction may succeed under another. In Figure 1 , a robot commanded to “put the green container in the bowl” instead places the tape in the bowl. This rollout fails for g = “put the green container in the bowl” g=\text{``put the green container in the bowl''} but succeeds for the hindsight instruction g ′ = “put the tape in the bowl” g^{\prime}=\text{``put the tape in the bowl''} . LfH relabels failed trajectories with instructions describing what the robot actually accomplished, converting zero-reward samples ( τ , g ) (\tau,g) into successful examples ( τ , g ′ ) (\tau,g^{\prime}) . The hindsight instruction g ′ g^{\prime} need not be drawn from P g P_{g} . It shares objects and skills with the commanded task, which the policy can later compose to solve that task, and in the low-success regime it provides signal where the commanded instruction gives none. LfH lets the policy learn from what it already manages to do before it can reliably solve the commanded tasks. LfH also escapes the weak initial policy with zero success rate and reaches 60% success by training step 60. LfH recovers useful training data from failed rollouts. The initial policy has zero success on the target task. As a result, standard GRPO assigns nearly all trajectory groups all-zero rewards and discards them, as shown in Figure 2 (a). In contrast, LfH retains nearly 80% of groups after hindsight relabeling. The remaining groups are discarded when the failures cannot be assigned a meaningful hindsight instruction and reward. LfH improves learning under sparse rewards. These recovered training examples allow GRPO to update even before the policy succeeds on the original task. Although the policy initially fails at “close the microwave,” its rollouts still contain behaviors that can be relabeled with meaningful instructions, such as “Put the mug inside the microwave” and “Pull the microwave door open.” These examples train the policy on objects and actions shared with the target task, including the microwave , the door , and the concept open , which is the opposite of close . We hypothesize that this allows the policy to reuse concepts learned from other relabeled instruction-trajectory pairs when learning the target instruction. 5.2 Can LfH improve sample efficiency? Setup. We evaluate whether LfH can accelerate RL fine-tuning when the initial VLA policy is weak. To this end, we construct out-of-distribution (OOD) tasks from the LIBERO-PRO benchmark using its task perturbation. These tasks preserve the original scenes, object instances, and environment dynamics, but change the task specification, such as the instructed object, target state, or desired action. For example, a policy may have seen the same kitchen scene during SFT, but must now manipulate a new object or reach a different goal state. This setting isolates the central challenge of sample-efficient RL: improving from mostly failed rollouts. Implementation & Baselines. We train each method separately on each of the four LIBERO-PRO suites (Spatial, Goal, Object, and Long), each containing 10 tasks, with task perturbations. We report results averaged across suites. Each run consists of 40 training steps, collecting 64 environment rollouts per step (8 groups of 8) for Spatial, Goal, and Object, and 512 rollouts per step (64 groups of 8) for Long. We report means and standard errors over four random seeds. We compare LfH with standard GRPO and GRPO with RoboMETER dense progress rewards [ 11 ] . Standard GRPO uses the sparse environment success reward for the original instruction, while GRPO+RoboMETER uses a dense reward measuring progress toward the same instruction. The latter tests whether denser reward feedback alone can account for the sample-efficiency gains of LfH. Metric. During training, we periodically freeze the policy and evaluate it with 480 rollouts per suite. We compute normalized gain separately for each task and then macro-average across tasks, giving each task equal weight. Let SR 0 \mathrm{SR}_{0} denote the success rate of the initial policy, and let SR t \mathrm{SR}_{t} denote the evaluation success rate after t t training steps. We define Gain ⁡ ( t ) = SR t SR 0 − 1 . \mathrm{Gain}(t)=\frac{\mathrm{SR}_{t}}{\mathrm{SR}_{0}}-1. This metric measures how quickly each method improves relative to the initial checkpoint as training proceeds. Every LIBERO-PRO task in this experiment has SR 0 > 0 \mathrm{SR}_{0}>0 , so the gain is always defined. The real-world task starts at zero success, so there we report raw success rates. Results. Figure 3(a) demonstrates that LfH substantially improves sample efficiency. LfH matches GRPO’s plateau performance after only 6 training steps, whereas GRPO requires approximately 30 steps, a 5 × 5\times reduction in environment interactions. LfH also outperforms GRPO with RoboMETER dense rewards, suggesting that denser feedback alone is insufficient when the policy rarely makes progress toward the commanded instruction. By step 40, LfH outperforms GRPO across all four suites. Figure 3(b) helps explain this gain. Following the default in RLinf [ 26 ] , groups whose mean reward falls below a lower threshold or above an upper threshold are filtered out before the update, since their near-uniform rewards yield vanishing advantages in Eq. ( 1 ). Under this filter, GRPO and GRPO+RoboMETER retain only 20–40% of rollout groups. LfH retains 70–80%, because relabeling turns many filtered all-failure groups into hindsight groups with informative rewards. Can more updates per rollout match LfH? We increase the number of update epochs for both GRPO and proximal policy optimization (PPO) [ 20 ] , meaning that each method takes more gradient steps on every rollout batch. The GRPO sweep directly tests whether extra optimization can explain LfH’s gain, while PPO provides the same test with another standard RL algorithm. Figure 7 shows that more updates do not consistently help either method and can hurt; even the best variants remain below LfH. Thus, LfH benefits from extracting new supervision from failed trajectories, not simply from reusing the same data more often. Generality. Finally, we test whether this benefit transfers beyond a single VLA backbone. We keep the suites, task perturbation, rollouts per step, and seeds from the π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} experiments. GR00T [ 2 ] and OpenVLA-OFT [ 9 ] improve more slowly under GRPO, so we train each until its GRPO baseline plateaus, which takes 200 steps for GR00T and 60 steps for OpenVLA-OFT. Figure 4: Comparison of LfH with ablations (4 seeds each) that modify parts of the hindsight mechanism. LfH outperforms rephrasing, reward-only relabeling, and random-reward. Can LfH performance gains be attributed to instruction augmentation or reward relabeling alone? We isolate the two steps in LfH: hindsight instruction relabeling and reward relabeling (Section 4 ). We compare against two ablations. Rephrase-only replaces the hindsight instruction g ′ g^{\prime} with a paraphrase of the original instruction g g while keeping the original rewards, testing whether language augmentation alone explains the gain. Reward-only keeps the instruction as g g but relabels rewards with the VLM, testing whether reward relabeling alone explains the gain. Both ablations improve only marginally over GRPO and stay well below LfH throughout training (Figure 4 ), so the benefit comes from coupling the two steps. Can LfH performance gains be attributed to perturbed rewards? We next test whether LfH works simply because it injects reward variance into otherwise discarded groups. The random-reward baseline creates nonzero advantages without semantically meaningful hindsight labels, yet gains little (Figure 4 ). The benefit of LfH therefore comes from grounded hindsight labels, not from avoiding zero-variance groups. When does LfH help the most? LfH is most effective when failures are diverse and semantically interpretable. Figure 9 shows sample rollouts at training step 40 on the microwave task. Standard GRPO produces many similar trajectories: the robot repeatedly approaches the same mug with similar gripper poses, leading to a low-diversity failure mode. In contrast, LfH rollouts show more varied object interactions and spatial configurations around the objects. Because these samples come after training has progressed, LfH may also help maintain behavioral diversity during RL fine-tuning, not only exploit diversity the initial policy already had. One possible explanation is that hindsight relabeling gives credit to multiple meaningful alternative behaviors, whereas GRPO treats them all as failures under the commanded task. As a result, GRPO can collapse toward repeated unsuccessful behaviors, while LfH continues to reinforce a broader set of grounded object interactions. How does data diversity affect LfH? We vary π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} ’s rollout noise to control trajectory diversity. Figure 8 shows an optimum for both methods: too little noise limits exploration, while too much produces incoherent rollouts. LfH benefits more near this optimum because it can relabel diverse failures as meaningful alternative tasks, whereas GRPO benefits only when exploration improves reward on the commanded task. 5.4 Real-world results Setup. Figure 5 shows that LfH improves real-world sample efficiency. Both methods start from zero, but LfH improves faster. At 128 rollouts, LfH achieves roughly twice the success rate of GRPO; at 160 rollouts, LfH reaches 56 % 56\% success, while GRPO plateaus at 22 % 22\% . Analysis. The initial policy often grounds instructions to the wrong object, for example grasping the tape regardless of the commanded instruction. GRPO treats these rollouts only as failures. LfH instead relabels them with instructions describing the executed behavior, such as “pick up the tape” or “move the tape to the bowl,” turning failed target-task attempts into useful supervision for object grounding and manipulation. 6 Discussion and Conclusion More bits per robot sample. LfH is motivated by a simple question: how many useful bits do we extract from each costly robot rollout? Classic RL uses a rollout mainly to evaluate whether the intended outcome happened, often reducing a rich trajectory to one reward. This is inefficient when physical interaction is expensive. Following the broader view that learning systems should extract more information from each sample [ 10 ] , LfH treats a trajectory as evidence for multiple language goals consistent with what happened. In this sense, hindsight relabeling increases the bits per sample: one rollout can supervise multiple possible tasks, rather than only the task originally commanded. Practical implication. This is especially natural for pretrained VLAs. Because these models already generalize across related language instructions, training on what the robot actually did can improve what it was asked to do. LfH therefore changes the role of robot data: failed attempts are no longer merely negative examples, but reusable experience that can be mined for nearby tasks. This offers a practical path for VLA post-training: instead of only collecting more rollouts or taking more optimization steps on the same labels, we can reinterpret each rollout through language and transfer learning across the tasks it supports. Limitations. LfH is limited by the behaviors present in the collected data. If the policy produces repetitive or uninformative trajectories, there are few meaningful hindsight goals to recover. It also depends on relabeling quality, since incorrect or overly generic instructions introduce noisy supervision. Better filtering, uncertainty estimation, and lightweight human verification are important directions for reliable deployment. We relabel only groups whose mean original-task reward falls below the threshold η = 0.8 \eta=0.8 , unless otherwise specified. This targets groups with enough failures to yield useful hindsight supervision while avoiding the compute and noisy or redundant updates caused by relabeling successful groups. Figure 6(a) compares our 0.8 threshold with two variants: random selection of the same 70% of groups and relabeling all groups. Thresholding performs substantially better than both. Random selection wastes VLM queries on uninformative groups, while relabeling all groups can dilute useful original-task supervision with redundant or noisy hindsight updates. We also ablate the threshold used for filtering groups. As shown in Figure 6(b) , a very low threshold, such as 0.1, relabels only all-failed groups and therefore misses many trajectories that fail the commanded task but still contain meaningful, reusable object interactions. A more moderate threshold, such as 0.5, improves over this stricter setting but still underperforms the default threshold of 0.8. LfH thus benefits most from relabeling a broad set of non-saturated groups. VLM prompt. LfH uses two structured templates. For instruction relabeling, the VLM receives a rollout and a list of scene objects, then returns a concise instruction describing the achieved behavior or Nothing if no meaningful interaction occurred. For reward relabeling, it receives the rollout and proposed hindsight instruction, then classifies completion as success, failure, or unsure , mapped to rewards 1, 0, and 0.5, respectively. On LIBERO-PRO Goal with task perturbation using π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} , we ablate the object list and the two abstention options. Figure 6(c) shows that removing any component reduces sample efficiency. Without the object list, labels become less grounded and consistent; without Nothing , idle or contact-free rollouts become noisy positive examples; without unsure , ambiguous videos receive potentially incorrect binary rewards. Thus, effective relabeling requires prompts that constrain labels and permit filtering or abstention. Weaker VLM. Trajectories are grouped into sets of K = 8 K{=}8 . Groups whose mean reward falls within [ θ low , θ high ] = [ 0.8 , 1.0 ] [\theta_{\text{low}},\theta_{\text{high}}]=[0.8,1.0] are skipped; all other groups (mean reward < 0.8 <0.8 ) are selected for hindsight relabeling. This focuses relabeling on groups with substantial failure rates where hindsight instructions provide the most value, while leaving near-saturated groups untouched. Hindsight loss clipping. LfH can occasionally produce very large hindsight-policy losses, especially early in training when hindsight rewards are noisy. To prevent a small number of hindsight groups from dominating optimization, we clip the hindsight GRPO loss before applying the hindsight weight: L LfH ​ ( θ ) = L GRPO ​ ( θ ) + λ ⋅ min ⁡ ( L H ​ - ​ GRPO ​ ( θ ) , c ) , L_{\mathrm{LfH}}(\theta)=L_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\theta)+\lambda\cdot\min\left(L_{\mathrm{H\text{-}GRPO}}(\theta),c\right), where c c is a clipping threshold and λ \lambda controls the overall contribution of hindsight updates. This bounds the maximum contribution of the hindsight term to λ ​ c \lambda c , while leaving the standard GRPO objective unchanged. We keep this maximum hindsight contribution fixed across all experiments. To set the clipping threshold, we estimate the empirical distribution of hindsight losses during the early stage of training and choose c c as approximately the 85th percentile. We then choose λ \lambda such that the weighted LfH batch loss is roughly equal to the original batch policy loss. Same-instruction skip. Before reward evaluation, any trajectory whose relabeled instruction matches the original task (after case/punctuation normalization) reverts to its original instruction and environment reward, avoiding redundant VLM queries.
